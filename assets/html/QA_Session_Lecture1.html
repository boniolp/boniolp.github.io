Q1

What is Information Retrieval?

How would you define Information Retrieval (IR)? Give an example of an IR system you use every day.

Information Retrieval is the task of identifying and retrieving information that is relevant to an information need, usually expressed as a search query. The system returns a ranked list of results ordered by relevance.

Examples: web search engines (Google, Bing), email search, Google Scholar. The core challenge is measuring relevance — what makes a document a good match for a query?

Q2

Generalising the F-score

An IR system returns 80 documents; 60 are relevant. The database contains 200 relevant documents.

(a) Compute Precision $P$ and Recall $R$.

(b) The $F_1$ score is the harmonic mean of $P$ and $R$: $F_1 = \dfrac{2PR}{P+R}$. Propose a general family $F_\beta$ where $\beta$ controls how much more important recall is than precision. Derive from the weighted harmonic mean.

(c) In a medical triage system, surfacing an irrelevant document is twice as costly as missing a relevant one. Find $\beta$ and compute the score.

(a)

$$P = \frac{60}{80} = 0.75 \qquad R = \frac{60}{200} = 0.30$$

(b)

he weighted harmonic mean of $P$ and $R$ with weights $w_P$ and $w_R$ is: $$H = \frac{w_P + w_R}{\dfrac{w_P}{P} + \dfrac{w_R}{R}}$$ Set $w_P=1$, $w_R=\beta^2$: $$F_\beta = \frac{(1+\beta^2)\,PR}{\beta^2 P + R}$$ Check: $\beta=1 \Rightarrow F_1=\dfrac{2PR}{P+R}$ ✓

(c)

Precision should carry twice the weight of recall $\Rightarrow \beta^2=\tfrac{1}{2} \Rightarrow \beta=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\approx 0.707$. $$F_{1/\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1.5\times0.75\times0.30}{0.5\times0.75+0.30} = \frac{0.3375}{0.675} = 0.50$$

Q3

TF-IDF

What are the two principles behind TF-IDF? Why is it not enough to simply count how many times a word appears in a document?

  • Term Frequency (TF): the more a term appears in a document, the more representative it is.
  • Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): the less a term appears across the collection, the more discriminative it is.

Raw counts treat very common words and discriminative ones as equally informative. TF-IDF down-weights ubiquitous words: $$\text{tf-idf}(t,d) = \text{tf}(t,d)\times\log\!\left(\frac{|D|}{|\{d'\in D:\text{tf}(t,d')>0\}|}\right)$$

Q4

Which Similarity Measure Should You Use?

You want to rank documents by similarity to a query $q$. Each is a TF-IDF vector in $\mathbb{R}^V$. Three proposals:

  • A — Euclidean distance: $d_E(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2$
  • B — Normalised Euclidean: $\hat{x}=x/\|x\|_2$, then $d_N(x,y)=\|\hat{x}-\hat{y}\|_2$
  • C — Cosine similarity: $\cos(x,y)=\dfrac{x\cdot y}{\|x\|_2\|y\|_2}$

(a) Which measures are appropriate and which ones are not?

(a) Eliminating Euclidean distance

Let $D_2=2D_1$ (same topics, twice as long). Then $d_E(q,D_1)\neq d_E(q,D_2)$ in general — Euclidean distance has a length bias.

Both B and C are scale-invariant: for $\alpha,\beta>0$, $d_N(\alpha x,\beta y)=d_N(x,y)$ and $\cos(\alpha x,\beta y)=\cos(x,y)$.

(b) Identical rankings

Since $\hat{x},\hat{y}$ are unit vectors: $$d_N(x,y)^2=\|\hat{x}-\hat{y}\|_2^2=1+1-2\cos(x,y)$$ $$\boxed{d_N(x,y)=\sqrt{2(1-\cos(x,y))}}$$ The map $t\mapsto\sqrt{2(1-t)}$ is strictly decreasing, so ascending $d_N$ order equals descending $\cos$ order. Cosine is preferred — normalisation is handled in the formula itself.

Q5

The Limits of TF-IDF for the Web

Is using cosine similarity on TF-IDF representation an appropriate choice for ranking web documents based on relevance? Cite at least two alternatives.

Keyword stuffing: inserting many repetitions of keywords (sometimes hidden) to inflate TF-IDF scores without genuine content. TF-IDF only sees word frequencies — it cannot distinguish a quality article from a keyword-stuffed page.

(Klienberg, 1998) and (Page & Brin, 1998) uses the web's link structure: a link from A to B is an endorsement. Pages receiving many links from authoritative pages are ranked higher.

Q6

About PageRank

PageRank iterates $\pi\leftarrow M\pi$ where $M=d\tilde{G}+(1-d)U$. Here $G$ is the transition matrix: $G_{ij}=1/\deg^+(j)$ if $j\to i$, else $0$ (the column-normalised transpose of the adjacency matrix). $\tilde{G}$ is $G$ with dangling nodes repaired (i.e., probability of leaving a dangling node is uniform to any other node in the graph).

(a) Prove $M$ is column-stochastic and conclude $\pi$ stays a valid probability vector.

(b) Give a 3-node example where raw $G$ causes the computation to fail, with numerical evidence.

(a)

Recall how $\tilde{G}$ is built from the raw transition matrix $G$ ($G_{ij} = 1/\deg^+(j)$ if $j \to i$, else $0$): $$\tilde{G}_{ij} = \begin{cases} G_{ij} & \text{if page } j \text{ has at least one outgoing link} \\ 1/N & \text{if page } j \text{ is a dangling node (column of } G \text{ is all-zero)} \end{cases}$$ For non-dangling pages, column $j$ of $G$ already sums to 1 (it is a probability distribution over destinations). For dangling pages, the column is replaced by the uniform distribution $\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}$, which also sums to 1. In both cases every column of $\tilde{G}$ sums to 1 and all entries are $\geq 0$, so $\tilde{G}$ is column-stochastic. Every column of $U$ sums to $N \cdot \tfrac{1}{N} = 1$, so $U$ is column-stochastic. For column $j$ of $M$: $$\mathbf{1}^\top M_{:j} = d\,\underbrace{\mathbf{1}^\top \tilde{G}_{:j}}_{=\,1} + (1-d)\,\underbrace{\mathbf{1}^\top U_{:j}}_{=\,1} = d + (1-d) = 1$$ All entries of $M$ are non-negative ($d \geq 0$, $1-d \geq 0$, $\tilde{G}_{ij} \geq 0$, $U_{ij} > 0$). Hence $M$ is column-stochastic. If $\pi^{(k)}$ is a probability vector ($\mathbf{1}^\top \pi^{(k)} = 1$, entries $\geq 0$), then: $$\mathbf{1}^\top \pi^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{1}^\top M \pi^{(k)} = (\mathbf{1}^\top M)\,\pi^{(k)} = \mathbf{1}^\top \pi^{(k)} = 1$$ because every entry of $\mathbf{1}^\top M$ equals 1. The iteration preserves total probability mass, and the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees convergence to a unique stationary distribution.

(b)

Three pages: pages 1 and 2 each link only to page 3, and page 3 is dangling (no outgoing links). The transition matrix $G$ (where $G_{ij} = 1/\deg^+(j)$ if $j \to i$, and note this is the transpose of the usual adjacency matrix) is: $$G = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$ Columns 1 and 2 each sum to 1 (each page has one outgoing link to page 3). Column 3 sums to 0 — not column-stochastic. Form $M_{\text{bad}} = d\,G + (1-d)\,U$ with $d = 0.85$: $$\text{column 3 of } M_{\text{bad}} = 0.85 \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} + 0.15\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\1/3\\1/3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.05\\0.05\\0.05\end{pmatrix} \quad \Rightarrow \text{sum} = 0.15 \neq 1$$ Start from $\pi_0 = \bigl[\tfrac{1}{3},\,\tfrac{1}{3},\,\tfrac{1}{3}\bigr]^\top$. After one step: $$G\pi_0 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\\tfrac{1}{3}+\tfrac{1}{3}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\\tfrac{2}{3}\end{pmatrix} \quad \text{(the \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) mass concentrated on dangling page 3 vanishes!)}$$ $$\pi_1 = 0.85\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\\tfrac{2}{3}\end{pmatrix} + 0.15\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{3}\\\tfrac{1}{3}\\\tfrac{1}{3}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.05\\0.05\\0.617\end{pmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{1}^\top\pi_1 \approx 0.717 \neq 1$$

Q7

Web Crawling Basics

What are the two fundamental questions any web crawler must answer? What data structures are used to address each one?

  • What pages to add to the frontier? — Specifically, how to avoid revisiting the same page twice. This is solved with a hash set of visited URLs: hashing gives O(1) average-case lookup, so checking membership in a collection of N URLs costs O(1) rather than O(N) for a direct search.
  • Which page to crawl next? — This is the traversal-order decision. Two classical strategies:
    • BFS uses a FIFO queue. It discovers pages level by level — all pages at distance 1 before distance 2, etc. Preferred for web crawling because it finds high-level pages (which tend to be broadly linked) early.
    • DFS uses a stack (LIFO). It dives deep into one branch before backtracking. Poor for web crawling because it can get stuck exploring one corner of the web indefinitely.
Q8

MinHash vs. Random Sampling of Shingles

To estimate Jaccard $J(A,B)=|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$, two students propose:

  • Student 1 (Sampling): Draw samples $S_A,S_B$ of size $k$. Estimate $\hat{J}_S=|S_A\cap S_B|/|S_A\cup S_B|$.
  • Student 2 (MinHash): Use $k$ hash functions; estimate $\hat{J}_{\text{MH}}=\tfrac{1}{k}\sum_i\mathbf{1}[m_i(A)=m_i(B)]$.

(a) Prove $\Pr[m(A)=m(B)]=J(A,B)$ for one MinHash function.

(b) Derive $\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{J}_{\text{MH}})$ and show MinHash converges to $J$.

(c) Let $n=|A|=|B|$. Derive the bias of $\hat{J}_S$ and show MinHash converges faster.

(a)

Model $h$ as a random permutation. The minimum over $A\cup B$ is equally likely to be any element; it satisfies $m(A)=m(B)$ iff it lies in $A\cap B$: $$\Pr[m(A)=m(B)]=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}=J\quad\checkmark$$

(b)

With $k$ independent hash functions, $\hat{J}_{\text{MH}} = \tfrac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k \mathbf{1}[m_i(A) = m_i(B)]$ is the average of $k$ different 0-1 random variables, each of which is one when $m_i(A) = m_i(B)$ and zero otherwise: $$\mathbb{E}[\hat{J}_{\text{MH}}] = J \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{Bias} = 0$$ $$\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{J}_{\text{MH}}) = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{J}_{\text{MH}}) = \frac{J(1-J)}{k}$$ The MSE vanishes as $k \to \infty$ independently of $|A|$ and $|B|$: to achieve $\operatorname{MSE} \leq \varepsilon^2$, it suffices to take $k \geq J(1-J)/\varepsilon^2$.

(c)

Let $|A| = |B| = n$, $|A \cap B| = m$, $|A \setminus B| = |B \setminus A| = n - m$, so $J = m/(2n-m)$.

(c.1) Expected numerator

Consider an element $x \in A \cap B$. The two samples are drawn independently: $S_A$ from $A$ and $S_B$ from $B$. Since $x$ belongs to both sets, it can be selected by either draw: $$\Pr[x \in S_A] = \frac{k}{n}, \qquad \Pr[x \in S_B] = \frac{k}{n}$$ For $x$ to land in the **intersection** $S_A \cap S_B$ it must be selected by **both** independent draws: $$\Pr[x \in S_A \cap S_B] = \frac{k}{n} \cdot \frac{k}{n} = \frac{k^2}{n^2}$$ Elements in $A \setminus B$ can never enter $S_B$, and elements in $B \setminus A$ can never enter $S_A$, so they contribute $0$ to the intersection. By linearity of expectation: $$\mathbb{E}[|S_A \cap S_B|] = m \cdot \frac{k^2}{n^2}$$

(c.2) Expected denominator

We always draw exactly $k$ elements into $S_A$ and $k$ into $S_B$. Every element we pick is by definition in the union, so by inclusion–exclusion on the two fixed-size samples: $$|S_A \cup S_B| = |S_A| + |S_B| - |S_A \cap S_B| = 2k - |S_A \cap S_B|$$ Taking expectations: $$\mathbb{E}[|S_A \cup S_B|] = 2k - \mathbb{E}[|S_A \cap S_B|] = 2k - \frac{mk^2}{n^2}$$ For $k \ll n$ the second-order term $mk^2/n^2$ is negligible, so: $$\mathbb{E}[\hat{J}_{\text{S}}] \approx \frac{mk^2/n^2}{2k} = \frac{mk}{2n^2}$$ Since $J \approx m/(2n)$ (for $m \ll n$), this simplifies to: $$\mathbb{E}[\hat{J}_{\text{S}}] \approx J \cdot \frac{k}{n}$$

MinHash's entire error is variance (zero bias), shrinking as $1/k$ regardless of $n$. Sampling has an irreducible bias that only vanishes when $k\approx n$.

Q9

Crawl Ordering Policy

Define weighted coverage $\text{WC}(t)=\sum_{p\in R(t)}w(p)$ where $R(t)$ is the set of pages crawled by time $t$.

(a) For general-purpose indexing, propose a principled choice of $w(p)$ and justify it.

(b) What practical complication arises, and how can it be worked around?

(a)

Set $w(p)=\text{PR}(p)$. PageRank captures universal authority independently of topic — pages that many authoritative pages link to are crawled first, front-loading the highest-value content within a limited budget.

(b)

PageRank requires the full link graph, which is still being built. Two workarounds:

  • Periodic recomputation on the partial graph, reordering the frontier every $K$ pages.
  • OPIC: Consider an online estimation of Page Rank.
Q10

Designing a Wikipedia Path Crawler

You want to find a path from "Python (Programming language)" to "Edgar F. Codd" on Wikipedia. You can only start from the first node (i.e., Python Wikipedia page).:

  • What kind of graph does Wikipedia form?
  • Propose a solution to find the shortest path shortest.

Part A — Discussion

Key ideas
  • Wikipedia is a directed graph: pages → nodes, hyperlinks → directed edges.
  • BFS guarantees the shortest path (fewest hops); explores all pages at depth $d$ before $d+1$. But such solution might visit too much pages before reaching the target.
  • A heuristic uses cosine similarity to the target and sort the crawl list according to the relevance.
